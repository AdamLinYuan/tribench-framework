Abstract




Recommender systems research and development often rely on complex datasets and the use of numerical metrics to compare di!erent recommender systems. Past solutions have su!ered from poor visualisation performance, the lack of real-time interactivity, and non-intuitive representa-tion of data. RecViz is a web application developed to address these shortcomings. It provides to the user force-directed graph visualisation of recommender systems datasets, that can be easier to navigate and understand compared to other visualisation formats. A supervised user evaluation with 10 users showed that 90% of participants understood RecViz visualisations well, and 90% of participants also thought that RecViz could be a useful tool for comparing recommender systems.

By leveraging modern GPU acceleration via CUDA, RecViz brings fast force-directed graph layout at scale to recommender systems use cases.

!  Introduction




This chapter outlines the motivation for the development of RecViz, a web application for the visualisation of recommendation datasets and the output of recommender systems. Key challenges comprising the problem space are highlighted, and the overall aims of the project are introduced.


1.1	Motivations

Recommender systems, which suggest items to users based on their predicted relevance and interest to the users, have played an enabling role in the continued growth of content-driven and data-driven experiences across multiple domains (Raza et al. 2024). The massive volumes of data that users now interact with mean that recommender systems have become indispensable tools for personalising user experiences, as well as helping users ﬁnd what they want faster by acting as information ﬁlters (Roy and Dutta 2022).
Correspondingly, datasets that are commonly used to aid recommender system research and development can contain hundreds of thousands or even millions of user-item interactions. The performance of recommender systems is measured primarily through numerical evaluation metrics such as NDCG, precision, and recall (Jadon and Patil 2023), which provide insights into the recommendation quality in an aggregate manner (i.e. across many users).
These metrics are useful for evaluation and comparison between di!erent systems, but inherently lack granularity (McNee et al. 2006). For example, these metrics do not necessarily or easily allow inspection of recommendations on a per-user basis, and typically obscure known attributes of items (e.g. name, release date) and users (e.g. age, occupation).
While this information could be manually extracted, or automatically extracted by writing complex scripts, this process can be time-consuming, especially if a researcher or developer lacks a su"cient data science background. Being able to get to this information in a quicker and easier way could reduce the amount of time required to improve a recommender system in development or better understand the behaviour of an existing one.
The scale of these datasets poses its own challenges. Interactive and even static visualisation of large datasets in general has proved to be computationally problematic, and ill-supported by mainstream visualisation software (Agrawal et al. 2015). Although there have been numerous attempts in the past to visualise recommender systems and the associated datasets (Zuo et al. 2019), these often show highly restricted subsets of information, in the order of hundreds of entities.
There is clear potential in better exploiting modern hardware, such as GPUs, to push boundaries on interactivity, responsiveness, and scale of visualisation.


1.2	Aims

This project proposes to develop RecViz, a novel web application that will target recommender systems researchers and developers as its two main user groups. In order to aid these groups, the application will aim to facilitate:
2


1.  Accessible and clear visualisation of recommendation datasets 2.  The predictions of recommender systems on these datasets
3.  Comparison of predictions between di!erent recommender systems or models
RecViz will address these overarching aims by implementing features such as side-by-side visuali-sations for di!erent recommender systems, adjustable visualisation detail levels, and responsive interactivity.
Application users should be able to easily choose speciﬁc dataset items or users to analyse and compare the behaviour of recommender systems acting upon them. They should also be able to choose between di!erent levels of visual abstraction to keep cognitive load of end-users under control (Viola et al. 2019), while retaining the ability to view entire datasets simultaneously. It should be clear how to set up the application and add or remove di!erent recommender systems from the analysis pool.
Additionally, the application should aim to be performant on systems with mid-range or better hardware and mainstream web browsers. The application must be designed in a maintainable, extensible manner through clear separation of concerns, robust testing, and a modular design. A modern technology stack will be used to maximise scalability, performance, and future-prooﬁng of the architecture. The application will be thoroughly evaluated on its ability to help end-users complete useful workﬂows and better understand deployed recommender models.
The underlying architecture of RecViz should be well-documented to support future work, and the user interface should be well-documented and have an intuitive and visually appealing design, in order to reduce the learning curve for onboarding new users.


1.3	Outline

The next chapters of this paper will build upon the concept of RecViz and the introduced motivation and aims, by providing more detail on the design and development process, and evaluating the extent to which value was provided for the application users.
•  Chapter 2: provides an overview of related past projects / papers from which the project has drawn inspiration, along with an assessment of their successes and shortcomings.

•  Chapter 7: summarises what was achieved, proposes avenues for future work, and reﬂects on the project overall.

"  Background




In this chapter, we describe previous work that provides related functionality to that envisioned for RecViz and that fed into the design process.  Prior successes informed what elements the RecViz project should try to build upon, and the identiﬁed shortcomings helped build a more valuable feature set.



2.1	Existing Work

Parts of this section may refer to the concept of visualisation idioms (Zuo et al. 2019), which will allow us to distinguish between relevant work.

2.1.1	SmallWorlds

SmallWorlds was a social recommender system, consisting of a Facebook application that used interactive graph-based visualisations, shown in Figure 2.1 as a control mechanism for an au-tomated collaborative ﬁltering (ACF) algorithm, in order to generate item recommendations for the user, based on the similarity of their friend’s proﬁle characteristics compared to items. In this case, items were a mixture of books, music, and movies, but other entities provided by the Facebook API were supported. (Gretarsson et al. 2010).













Figure 2.1: Screenshot of the main "tree" interface of the SmallWorlds web application, with annotated layer structure. Figure from Gretarsson et al. (2010).

SmallWorlds visualised dataset users and items using the node-link diagram idiom. Two represen-tation options were provided for application users, one being a tree-like graph, and the other a circular layout. Both were generated by a modiﬁed version of the Fruchterman-Reingold graph layout algorithm and had ﬁve distinct layers, ranging from the actively selected user in the ﬁrst layer, through to layers for recommended and distant items respectively. The paper included a user study consisting of information discovery tasks and user satisfaction questions. It showed that both layouts were perceived as signiﬁcantly better than Facebook’s own site interface on several
4


user experience questions, such as information discoverability and intuitiveness. Respondents preferred the tree-like layout over the circular one in the majority of cases. Furthermore, the resultant recommendations from interaction were found to be similar to those generated by the ACF algorithm when given the user’s Facebook proﬁles as input.
The use of the node-link idiom combined with distinct layers of nodes, where higher node distances indicated dissimilarity, was clearly viable.  Despite this, two clear shortcomings of SmallWorlds showed include sub-optimal interaction performance (10-20 frames per second (FPS) despite displaying no more than a few hundred edges), and a reliance on server-side rendering to achieve that level of performance. The WiGis paper detailing the employed rendering method showed only around 0.2 FPS was achieved with a graph containing two million edges (Svetlin et al. 2010).
It is important to note that typical hardware has improved substantially since this paper was published. However, rough prototyping has indicated that even modern hardware struggles to achieve 10 FPS using methods based on SVG and HTML, comparable to those of WiGis. RecViz will aim much higher than this through the use of modern rendering methods in conjunction with modern hardware.

2.1.2	Interactive Visualization of Recommender Systems Data

Richthammer et al. (2017) proposed interactive visualisation as a valid technique for showing the output recommendations of a given system. A range of di!erent visualisations were explored, including bar charts of item ratings, rating matrices, word clouds for item reviews, and parallel coordinates, shown in Figure 2.2, for item features. The idea of recommender systems having "high non-transparency" referred to by Richthammer et al. meant that this research aimed at involving the user in the recommendation process rather than explaining algorithmic details behind how the recommendations were generated. Indeed, this concept was also a key factor in the requirements scope of RecViz, which the next chapter of this paper elaborates on.















Figure 2.2: Screenshot of candidate items visualisation, ranked by descending rating with parallel coordinates for item features. Figure from Richthammer et al. (2017).


In the research of Richthammer et al. (2017), the presented scenario, which illustrated an example "information reduction" process conducted with the help of parallel coordinates, is of particular interest. The use of a parallel coordinates overview to quickly ﬁnd item commonalities showed clear potential. Although worth exploring, a key pitfall of this idiom is that the number of edges in a parallel coordinates representation can become high enough to make readability poor for the application user. Also of interest was the use of node-order to determine communities, shown
5


by colour-coding in the rating matrix representation, although the readability of this idiom for large scale data is potentially problematic. RecViz will use edges similarly to Figure 2.2 in order to show commonalities between di!erent sets of user recommendations and ground truth sets.

2.1.3	Graph Embeddings for Movie Visualization & Recommendation

Vlachos et al. (2012) used a combination of a minimum spanning tree (MST), where movie distances were based on content similarity metrics relative to a user-deﬁned pivot item, and a dendrogram to represent clusters of nodes within the MST. The MST was then presented to the user via a web interface, with colour-coding according to the di!erent parts of the dendrogram, shown in Figure 2.3.











Figure 2.3: The visualisation of movie recommendations derived from the MST and dendrogram. Figure adapted from Vlachos et al. (2012).

The user interface and accompanying representation appeared e!ective in that the neighbourhood structure of the graph is clear, and it is easy for the user to identify similar movies. However, the research did not test the scalability of the approach when needing to show a larger number of similar movies. Additionally, requiring the user to choose a pivot item manually is not something RecViz would want to replicate, as in general there is a desire to avoid assuming that the application user knows much about the dataset in the use cases of RecViz. Being able to show communities of common movies is a unique feature that was in neither of the work of Gretarsson et al. (2010) or Vlachos et al. (2012). RecViz will employ community detection to provide the end-user with another way of reading node-link graphs.


2.2	Relevant Technologies

This section reviews visualisation technologies that either provided us valuable insight into large-scale data visualisation or showed clear potential for increasing the application’s performance with large-scale datasets.

2.2.1	Gephi

Gephi is a desktop application focusing on graph visualisation. It is written in Java and features a rendering engine based on OpenGL. This allows display of networks with up to 1 million edges (Gephi 2008), which is clearly a larger scale of visualisation than demonstrated by other existing work.  A number of useful graph utility functions are provided such as community detection and force-directed layout. The user interface, shown in Figure 2.4, features a timeline for scrubbing through timestamped data. Considering that several recommendation datasets contain timestamps, this leads to the inclusion of an equivalent feature in the requirements, as
6


it could help RecViz users gain insight into whether model performance is inﬂuenced or not inﬂuenced by data at speciﬁc timestamps.













Figure 2.4: A view of the Gephi user interface while visualising a network. Figure from Gephi (2008).

One clear drawback of Gephi is that it lacks programmable control of graph interactions, which in contrast would be crucial to RecViz, in order to tailor it to the desired use cases (Garcia-Robledo and Zangiabady 2023). Instead, there is a reliance on plugins for additional functionality outwith direct interaction with the graph. This would add excess user complexity in the case of RecViz and requires development of a plugin architecture.  A further shortcoming is that the utility functions do not utilise GPU acceleration, meaning it would take many minutes or even hours to run graph layout and community detection for networks exceeding 1 million edges.  For RecViz, there is a need for these algorithms to execute orders of magnitude faster to increase the viability of potential user workﬂows. RecViz will support increased e"ciency through the use of GPU-accelerated versions of these algorithms.

2.2.2	RAPIDS cuGraph

RAPIDS cuGraph is an open-source library exposing a collection of graph-related functions which are GPU-accelerated by CUDA (NVIDIA 2019). The library includes force-directed layout and community detection algorithms, making it very advantageous for RecViz use cases such as visualising user-item interactions in a meaningful way and identifying communities of similar users even in large datasets. Integration within RecViz could enable application users to re-generate visualisations and switch between di!erent datasets much more e"ciently.
Performance testing using an 80GB NVIDIA A100 GPU showed a 60x speedup running the Louvain community detection algorithm, with other algorithms showing up to a 485x speedup (NVIDIA 2019). However, a key drawback is that the library relies on NVIDIA GPUs, meaning not all potential users of RecViz would be able to beneﬁt from any performance gains - this is a problem that the application design aims to alleviate through the implementation of a graph caching feature.


2.3	Summary

Several relevant items of past work were discussed, expanding on their role in forming the initial basis of this project, prior to the requirements elicitation stage. This chapter weighed up their advantages and disadvantages in order to provide additional direction for meeting the project aims, and better understand their viability.

#  Analysis/Requirements




In this chapter, the elicitation process for a prioritised set of functional and non-functional requirements is detailed. A prototyping phase was undertaken, which along with supervisor discussions, caused modiﬁcations to the requirements set. This chapter details the ﬁnal set of these requirements, which were inﬂuenced by the performance shortcomings and layout of SmallWorlds in Section 2.1.1, the colour-coding of the products in Section 2.1.2 and Section 2.1.3, and the lack of GPU acceleration in Gephi (Gephi 2008), in addition to extensive discussion with the project supervisor and a recommender systems researcher to understand what developers and researchers would require.


3.1	User Scenarios

The ﬁrst elicitation step was to model the range of possible use cases of RecViz at a high-level, through writing detailed user scenarios corresponding to speciﬁc types of users. Note that the target audience would be mostly people doing recommender systems research and development.

•  Hannah is a developer of recommender systems. Hannah enters the visualisation layer and loads a dataset containing user-item interactions. She sees a graph that represents clusters of users with similar interaction histories. Using the ﬁlter/cluster panel, she segments the users by age. She then selects users aged 25-35 and narrows the item list to movies in the "Sci-Fi" genre. Intrigued by that speciﬁc user cluster, Hannah hovers over a node to view user details like interaction history and favourite genres. She selects two users from the cluster for deeper comparison in the recommendation layer.
•  James is a software engineer working on improving a recommendation algorithm. He imports datasets from di!erent domains (movies and books) into the application. After visualising the datasets, he compares two users with similar interaction histories.  The application computes the similarity of their top-k recommendations from two models. James exports the numbers to CSV, later using this information to inform development.
•  Emily is a recommender systems researcher looking to assess the quality of recommen-dations from a recommender system.  She picks a user from the graph to inspect the interaction history. She then picks another user that is similar to that user, and compares their recommendations, ﬁnding that both users who like a particular type of item have poor recommendations. Emily proceeds to use these types of insights as part of her research ﬁndings.
•  Michael is an academic who is completely new to researching recommender systems. He selects two users from the graphs and uses the recommendation comparison layer to view how two models rank items. He observes that one model performs better in terms of cosine similarity with a user’s historical preferences. Michael exports this to a CSV ﬁle for later comparison with other models on the same dataset.

3.2	User Stories

The user scenarios were then categorised under broad use cases, and the speciﬁc user types were merged into three groups, which were developer, researcher, and application user (to represent general use cases).

•  Dataset exploration
–  As a developer, I want to visualise a dataset as a graph so that I can identify clusters and relationships among users and items.
–  As a developer, I want to ﬁlter users and items based on attributes like age, occupation, genre, or title so that I can focus on relevant clusters.
–  As a researcher, I want to view user and item details by hovering over nodes so that I can quickly access important information.
–  As a researcher, I want to select two users for comparison so that I can evaluate their recommendations in detail.
•  Recommendation comparison
–  As a researcher, I want to compare the top-k recommendations from two models side by side so that I can analyse their performance.
–  As a researcher, I want to compute similarity metrics like overlap and cosine similarity between two users’ recommendations so that I can quantify their likeness.
–  As a software engineer, I want to compare a user’s recommendations against a ground truth so that I can assess the performance of a model.
–  As a software engineer, I want to visualise user and item embeddings so that I can understand the representational di!erences between models.
•  General
–  As an application user, I want the tool to handle large datasets e"ciently so that I can gain insights without delays or undue di"culty.
–  As an application user, I want the application to work reliably across major browsers so that I can use it seamlessly in my working environment.
–  As an application user, I want the application to be easy to set up so that I can get started quickly.


3.3	Requirements Set

The prioritised list of requirements is then derived from the user scenarios. These are preﬁxed with letters according to the MoSCoW prioritisation method (Consortium 2014):
•  M: must-have requirements that are required for the application to be viable
•  S: should-have requirements that are important but do not compromise viability if omitted •  C: could-have requirements that are desirable but of a lower importance
• W: "wont have this time" requirements, that may have provided value, but are outside the scope of this project due to time constraints or impracticality.

The following requirements were categorised by functionally distinct layers:
•  Dataset and user-item interactions layer
1.  M1: A clear visualisation of the overall composition of the datasets used (items and users)

2.  M2: The ability to work with multiple di!erent datasets (cross-domain)

$  Design




This chapter introduces the system architecture of RecViz, accompanied by the reasoning behind design decisions. It discusses the user interface design, along with the purpose that each major component plays in meeting the requirements. Furthermore, reﬂecting a ﬂexible approach to system design, it goes on to discuss the evolution of the technology stack from the prototyping phase up to the ﬁnal product, and the decision-making process that shaped this.


4.1	System Architecture

RecViz follows the two-tier architecture architecture model. Namely, the system, illustrated in Figure 4.1, has a presentation layer consisting of the frontend and relevant components, and an application layer consisting of various "service" components exposed via a web server. It was decided from the outset that RecViz would be a web application instead of a native application, as this provides inherent cross-platform compatibility, and given the time constraints of the project, allows time-consuming middleware development to be avoided, while still retaining a separation of concerns between frontend and backend.


























Figure 4.1: Overall view of the system architecture of RecViz.
11


A two-tier architecture was chosen to reﬂect the fact that the envisioned use cases of RecViz do not require persistent user data to be stored in a structured database. The underlying datasets that the users interact with and generate graphs from are purely static ﬁles with respect to the backend web server. Furthermore, employing this architecture directly helps in meeting requirement M15 as the increased separation of concerns and component-based structure (implemented through object-oriented programming) increases maintainability. Software artefacts that address multiple cross-cutting concerns have been observed to be less stable long-term (Conejero et al. 2012). An additional beneﬁt is that the system becomes more extensible owing to the lower coupling. The application layer contains a web server exposing narrow-purpose services through API endpoints.

4.1.1	Backend Services

Shown in Figure 4.1, the graph service’s purpose is to handle the association between graphs and datasets and return the correct graph corresponding to queries from the frontend. Additionally, the graph service will be responsible for running force-directed graph layout algorithms, provid-ing meaning to the node positions in order to make the datasets comprehensible. It is important to note that this task could have been done on the frontend client in theory, but the design has o#oaded this task to the backend. Further detail on the reasoning and beneﬁts of this is in Chap-ter 5, but the most important point is that we cannot use CUDA GPU-acceleration in the browser.

The recommendation service oversees the loading of di!erent recommender systems in the form of model ﬁles, and obtaining and outputting JSON-structured predictions from these, crucial for meeting requirements M7 and M9. In this design, the model ﬁles used by the rec-ommendation service are stored locally on the same machine as the backend server, due to the project time constraints, although it would be viable to swap this out for a structured database in future iterations of RecViz.

These services are accompanied by the dataset service, which comprises functionality for the management of datasets stored locally, the association between model ﬁles and datasets, checking the validity of datasets and their associated models in order to meet requirement M8, and loading raw datasets into a structured form with appropriate data structures to be ingested by the other services. Furthermore, the dataset contains important functionality for necessary for obtaining the ground truth for recommendations, to help meet requirement S4.

The similarity service exposes relevant utility functions for comparing the interactions in the datasets, and the interaction histories of di!erent users, contributing towards meeting require-ments M10, S4, C1, and C3.

Finally, the web server ties all the services together, facilitating communication between them and aggregating the output into the ﬁnal response to the frontend query.

4.1.2	Frontend and Presentation Layer Components

In Figure 4.1, this layer provides eight major frontend components based on the requirements detailed in Section 3.3, which will be principally responsible for enabling e!ective and e"cient user interactions with the system. Requirement M2 and M5 stipulate that two graphs would have to be shown side-by-side on the frontend, leading to two graph visualiser components in the architecture. In order to present the recommendation comparison layer to the user, the design includes two components that are encapsulated within a dashboard component. The ﬁrst is a top-k visu-aliser component that shows top-k recommended items, which are predicted for each selected user from the selected recommender model of each respective graph, in order to meet requirement M9.

Furthermore, related to requirement S4, this component will support representing the ground truth set of items alongside the top-k recommendations. The second is a metrics comparison component, that is responsible for fetching and displaying returned embedding representations and relevant similarity metrics calculated by the backend to fully meet requirements M10, S4, C3, and C4.

Finally, the help and documentation components are for presenting concise information to the user on how to operate the system and about what functionality is available.  While self-contained, these will have their visibility determined by the UI controls navbar component.


4.1.3	Node Layout

Another key characteristic of the interface is the use of a graph layout algorithm, ForceAtlas2 (Jacomy et al. 2014), on large scale datasets in order to give important meaning to the visualisation. The simple logic behind this is that if any given node is linked to a similar set of nodes, when compared to another node, then these nodes will end up relatively closer together after the layout algorithm is run for a large number of iterations. RecViz utilises an implementation of ForceAtlas2 with su"cient performance to be run for 1,500 iterations, even on million-scale graphs, and still maintain runtimes consistently under 1 minute on modern NVIDIA GPUs.
13


4.1.4	Caching

In order to further improve graph rendering performance, the backend graph service is designed with a caching feature that retains past graphs that have already been through the layout algorithm, based on the queries that were used to fetch them, to prevent the layout algorithm being unnecessarily re-executed when an identical graph is later fetched again. This feature is targeted at users who do not have a NVIDIA GPU, because if a given graph takes a long time to run the layout algorithm on, it is undesirable for the user to have to wait that time again. A side beneﬁt of this feature is that the graph cache can be transferred from a machine that supports GPU-accelerated layout to one that does not, allowing users lacking the necessary hardware to view the graphs without waiting.


4.2	User Interface Design

4.2.1	Design Principles

Blair-Early and Zender (2008) described an interface design framework as a set of parameters, which are desired qualities of the interface, combined with a set of principles, to increase the e!ectiveness of the interface.  For the purposes of RecViz, requirements M11 and M13 are considered to form the primary design parameters (reliability and performance). To complete the design framework, three fundamental principles are deﬁned to guide RecViz user interface design decisions, and introduce a degree of consistency, formality, and care into decision making, in order to encourage an end product that is commensurate to the importance of the user experience:
1.  Visual simplicity:  Ognjanovic (2018) concluded that higher levels of display clutter negatively impacted perceived usability. On this premise, the design aims for not only the lowest number of frontend components necessary to meet the requirements, but more importantly, each component is designed to present itself with a minimal number of visual elements, to decrease the cognitive load on the user, without compromising functionality.
2.  Emphasis: typical user interaction ﬂows should avoid the user losing their sense of place within the interface. Correspondingly, the design should make use of common techniques to reinforce context, such as colour coding, visual feedback, and modal UI elements adhering to the "lightbox" technique (i.e. we do background dimming in the ﬁnal UI), which helps focus the user of speciﬁc parts of a task (Hudson and Viswanadha 2009).
3.  Intuitiveness:  although providing detailed help and documentation will aid meeting requirement M12, in order to meet this requirement fully, the interface should be designed such that the user can quickly infer the necessary inputs required to perform common tasks. It follows that a visually attractive interface should be aimed for, to reduce any potential intimidation of the user. The intention is to minimise the learning curve of the application.

4.2.2	Interface Planning and Prototyping Phase

The user interface design process began with a planning and prototyping phase consisting of two processes that ran concurrently. These were low-ﬁdelity wireframing (important as low-ﬁdelity wireframes can be produced rapidly) and user ﬂow diagram construction.

The aim of this approach was primarily to ensure that the initial design adequately addressed the Must Have requirements of the project, but secondary aims included providing a clear de-velopment reference to mitigate the potential for a lack of direction during the implementation phase, as well as reducing the risk of a product being developed that would require signiﬁcant revision late in development, which would be potentially fatal given the time constraints the

4.3	Design of Technology Stack

Not counting the requirements, the technology stack was mostly inﬂuenced by the tier structure of the system architecture. At the very beginning of the design process, the prospect of RecViz being developed as a native desktop application, for example as a Java-based app was brieﬂy considered. However, this was decided against, in favour of developing a web application given the beneﬁt of lower development overhead, since di!erences between OS platforms are abstracted away. The ﬁrst version of the web-based design utilised a monolithic architecture where there was no logical separation between frontend and backend.

As initial proof-of-concept functional prototypes of RecViz were developed, it became in-creasingly clear that this architecture was inadequate for likely use cases and detrimental to maintainability and extensibility. Monolithic codebases can become challenging to understand (Kamisetty et al. 2023), slowing down the development process in the context of an already constrained timescale. Therefore, the system design was reworked into the ﬁnal two-tier archi-tecture.

4.3.1	Frontend Technology Stack

In the initial versions of the design, the frontend was provided entirely by the Django web frame-work and its HTML templating engine. Custom CSS was used for the user interface accompanied
19


by vanilla JavaScript to provide functionality. Early prototypes used the well-known JavaScript data visualisation library D3.js to display the graphs. D3.js was initially attractive as it is highly documented, includes built-in support for common user interactions such panning and zooming, and even supports a range of force-directed layout algorithms (Bockstock and Observable 2024) which were applicable to the project’s use case. However, it was later found by test visualising MovieLens 100K that the performance of the CPU-based force-directed layout algorithms in D3.js were insu"cient, with long runtimes for larger graphs that would have very negatively impacted the user experience.

Furthermore, it was found that running graph layout algorithms with MovieLens 100K in the browser caused the user interface to become unresponsive under load, and that this could not be resolved without introducing web workers and adding more complexity to the codebase. At the point that the frontend and backend were separated, this responsibility was shifted to the backend, and the frontend migrated to using Meta’s React frontend library along with Type-Script. React is speciﬁcally designed to build frontends consisting of components (Meta 2024), and therefore it was key to realising the modular system architecture. TypeScript is a strongly-typed superset of JavaScript, bringing advantages such as static type checking to catch bugs earlier (Microsoft 2021), saving development time. Additional development time was saved by using the Material UI component library, allowing the delivery of a higher quality interface than what would have likely been achieved using custom-developed components in the constrained timeframe.

The frontend also switched to using the SigmaJS visualisation library for the graph visualiser components. This design choice yielded signiﬁcant performance beneﬁts, critical for meeting requirement M13, because in contrast to D3.js using SVG-based rendering, which exhibited performance issues even with graphs of less than 50,000 edges, SigmaJS utilises WebGL for rendering, which is GPU-accelerated and allows for faster drawing of larger graphs (médialab and OuestWare 2024).

Angular, a TypeScript based web framework developed by Google, was also considered, but ultimately React was chosen due to a lower learning curve (Shevitz 2020) and a richer library of third-party components and plugins.

4.3.2	Backend Technology Stack

The earliest iterations of the design used Django, and this persisted even after moving away from the monolithic architecture primarily due to backend-related considerations.  Being a Python-based web framework (Django 2024) was perceived as an advantage, as many recom-mender system libraries such as RecBole and Surprise (Hug 2019) are also based on Python, and are reliant on Python packages common in machine learning such as PyTorch and NumPy. Therefore, having the backend framework based on the same language as the recommender systems eliminates the need to write substantial middleware for the framework to communicate with the recommender systems, further saving valuable development time.

In retrospect, Flask, which is another Python-based web framework, could have been used for the backend. The authors of Flask describe it as a lightweight framework (Pallets 2010). One advantage of Flask is that many parts of the framework can be omitted by not importing them, whereas in Django there is considerable boilerplate code that is never used by the application. Despite this, Django was retained in the design as Flask was identiﬁed as an alternative frame-work too late in development to warrant the required code refactoring and migration, and a Django backend would arguably be easier to expand in the future, for example due to more comprehensive built-in support for databases and an admin panel.
20



The graph service uses the networkx Python package, which is designed for the creation and manipulation of complex networks (NetworkX 2024), to turn data parsed from datasets into a coherent ﬁle format representing the graph for SigmaJS. Simply sending the graph data as JSON responses was initially tried, but this approach resulted in larger ﬁle sizes, and it would have been unwieldy to run layout and similarity algorithms without the structured node and edge representation that networkx provided. RAPIDS cuGraph (NVIDIA 2019) is used to run the ForceAtlas2 graph layout algorithm on the backend with full GPU acceleration via CUDA, resulting in order-of-magnitude speedups over CPU-based graph layout. More detail on why and how RAPIDS cuGraph was integrated into the backend is provided in Chapter 5.

The recommendation service uses the RecBole Python library, which includes implementations of over 90 common recommendation algorithms (RecBole 2025b), encompassing state-of-the-art models like GRU4RecCPR and SASRecCPR (Chang et al. 2024) as well as useful functionality including training of and running prediction on recommender models, and useful abstractions for top-k recommendation, embedding extraction, and dataset loading.

The similarity service uses some networkx similarity metrics and some self-implemented metrics. This is detailed further in Chapter 5. The dataset service mostly relies on self-designed classes and data structures. It was decided at this stage that a structured database was unnecessary and that local storage was su"cient to enable all use cases covered by the requirements in Chapter 3, especially considering that the application has no user authentication.


4.4	Summary

In this chapter, the components and structure comprising the RecViz system architecture were discussed in detail for both frontend and backend, and key parts of the user interface design process were highlighted including the set of principles used when designing the interface, the merits of the design methodology, and the reasoning behind speciﬁc choices. Finally, the evolution of and the decisions behind the technology stack were discussed in detail, justifying the rejection of technologies and the acceptance of others to arrive at the ﬁnal technology stack, which used React, TypeScript, Material UI, and SigmaJS in the frontend, and Django, RAPIDS cuGraph, networkx, and RecBole in the backend. The next chapter will look at the tools and processes followed to integrate all of these technologies together.

5.1	Software Engineering Practice

Similarly to the design process of the project, the implementation process followed was di!erent initially and changed in response to di"culties encountered at the beginning of the implemen-tation process. Upon reﬂection, the emergence of these di"culties was a net beneﬁt, as they caused more attention to be paid to the software engineering practices used than might otherwise have been the case. There was certainly a sub-usage of software engineering practices initially. Fortunately, given that the resulting problems occurred early, there was ample opportunity to correct.

5.1.1	Version control and DevOps

All parts of the codebase were managed by the Git version control system. A local repository was kept synchronised with a remote repository stored on GitLab, an online platform that provides DevOps features such as the ability to run continuous integration pipelines (GitLab 2025). A fundamental beneﬁt of using Git is the aggregation of sets of changes into commits, allowing easy rollback of the codebase to a previous state in the event of uncaught regressions being introduced into the local copy of the codebase.

Another beneﬁt of Git is the ability to split the project into branches so that potentially less stable changes could be isolated from the main branch. Branching was not taken advantage of at the start of the project, due to it being judged as an unnecessary overhead with only one developer, compared to a multi-developer team where the risk of breaking changes being introduced would be higher.

However, it was quickly found that introducing major features into the main branch occa-sionally left the application not only unstable, but in a completely inoperable state until the feature was su"ciently complete. This made it di"cult to demonstrate interim progress and introduced unnecessary complexity into the codebase when multiple features were being developed alongside each other, which slowed down development. Subsequent major features were restricted to their own branches before being reintegrated into the main branch once they were ready, which alleviated these problems.
22



GitLab was used to run a continuous integration pipeline containing ESLint, which is a JavaScript code linter. Linters are static analysis tools that ﬁnd and highlight issues such as non-conventional style and language anti-patterns. As ESLint is a JavaScript linter, typescript-eslint, a Node.js package, was used to add TypeScript related rules to the linter.

It was planned to include unit tests in the continuous integration pipeline, but they were not ultimately included due to project-speciﬁc impracticality. As we detail in Section 6.1, an extensive test suite was run manually before pushing. The pipeline would have taken too long to set up given the large number of dependencies in the project and the fact that the university GitLab runners are ephemeral, meaning dependencies would have to be reinstalled every time tests are run. Furthermore, the runners had insu"cient hardware speciﬁcations to run the application code without extensive mocking to reduce the runtime of the tested code.

A pragmatic approach was taken in which testing was done manually using a written series of steps within the application that evolved depending on what features were complete. When the project became su"ciently complex, making manual testing too slow, unit tests were developed. These were locally ran at each commit and this was e!ective enough to prevent uncaught regressions being introduced. The results and e!ectiveness of the unit tests are elaborated on in Chapter 6.

5.2.2	Dataset Service

The dataset service is the ﬁrst to be instantiated when the backend web server starts up. It requires the environment variable RECVIZ_DS_PATH, an absolute path to a directory where a number of dataset folders are stored. Environment variables were chosen over using a YAML conﬁgura-tion ﬁle as the application does not need to be given any ﬁle paths to read an environment variable.

The structure of a dataset folder is illustrated in Figure 5.2.  Dataset folders are expected by the service to contain "atomic ﬁles" used by RecBole datasets, which are delimited ﬁles represent-ing common recommendation data like interactions, users, and items. These ﬁles are analogous to an 𝐿 → 𝑀 table, with the ﬁrst line of each ﬁle containing n columns corresponding to features, and the remaining 𝐿 ↑ 1 lines containing the data records (RecBole 2025a).










Figure 5.2: An example structure of the MovieLens 100K dataset (GroupLens 1998) set up for RecViz.

An empty dictionary is declared to store the loaded datasets. In order to load in all the available datasets, the implementation iterates through all folders in the datasets folder. For each of these folders, a list is maintained of the absolute paths of any ﬁle with an extension matching that of an atomic ﬁle, such as .inter or .user.  It then checks for a subfolder named models. The absolute paths of any PyTorch model ﬁles with the .pth ﬁle extension are mapped in a dictionary, using the ﬁle name as a key.

This dictionary and the lists, along with the path of the dataset folder and the dataset name taken from the folder name, are then used to construct valid Dataset objects, which are mapped to the dataset names in the dictionary.

These objects, upon instantiation, associate model ﬁles to the dataset, then parse the user and item features and map them to the user and item IDs contained in the atomic ﬁles. Furthermore, .inter ﬁles are parsed in order to populate the interaction histories within the user mapping. Each Dataset object includes methods that can be called to retrieve user or item IDs, user features, interaction timestamps, the full user and item mappings. There is also a method to check that user IDs and item IDs are validly deﬁned in the constructed Dataset objects.

Finally, the dataset service itself contains methods for listing the available datasets, retrieving the loaded Dataset objects, and retrieving the available models for a given dataset.

The graph service contains a get_graph method that takes in a dataset name and optionally a dictionary of ﬁlter parameters as arguments. After construction, the Graph objects are mapped to the ﬁlename in the cache dictionary, with a method to retrieve the absolute path of the correct GEXF ﬁle being available.

The Graph object performs four key tasks when being constructed.  The ﬁrst is to prepare the nodes for being converted into GEXF format, by making a copy of the user and item map-pings returned from the Dataset object according to the dataset name passed to the constructor. The code then adds attributes into the nodes that will need to be read from the GEXF later when rendering the graphs, including initial x and y coordinates, size, shape, and labels.

The second task that is performed is to iterate through all users and items in the now mod-iﬁed mappings, and add these to a networkx graph object. At this stage the code also checks the ﬁlter parameters to make sure only nodes that fall into at least one of the ﬁlter ranges or match one of the values are added into the graph. Correspondingly, when edges are added for each interaction, they are only added if both the nodes on each end are still present in the graph after ﬁltering.

The third task is to layout the nodes, thereby assigning them x and y-coordinates that have meaning. The implementation uses ForceAtlas2, an algorithm which simulates the application of physical forces to the nodes based on their connections, through applying a combination of formulas to each pair of nodes such as degree-dependent attraction and repulsion, as well as gravity (Jacomy et al. 2014).

In order to produce a reasonable layout, all the relevant formulae must be applied to the graph for many iterations to allow su"cient opportunity for nodes to be a!ected by the forces. The RecViz graph service applies ForceAtlas2 for 1,500 iterations, a number which appeared to produce meaningful layouts even with larger datasets. 2,000, 4,000, and 8,000 iterations were tested and no meaningful beneﬁt observed.

Applying the required formulae for layout to the graph is too computationally intensive to be done with a runtime that is reasonable for quick analysis of the datasets, shown by typical ForceAtlas2 implementations, including those used in networkx, D3.js, and SigmaJS exhibiting runtimes of between ﬁve and ten minutes for the MovieLens 100K dataset during testing, and not completing despite being allowed three hours of runtime for the MovieLens 1M dataset (GroupLens 2003). Some exact runtimes are provided in Chapter 6.

Even with the implementation of the Barnes-Hut optimisation for more e"cient repulsion resulting in a average-case time complexity of 𝑆(𝑀𝑇𝑈𝑅𝑀) (Barnes and Hut 1986), the ForceAtlas2 algorithm generally su!ers from runtimes of several hours in networks exceeding 100,000 nodes (Jacomy et al. 2014). This refers to the typical case where ForceAtlas2 is run on a single CPU thread.

A key technical achievement of the RecViz implementation is the integration of CUDA-accelerated ForceAtlas2 from RAPIDS cuGraph, outlined in Section 2.2.2. RAPIDS cuGraph allows RecViz to take advantage of parallelisation for ForceAtlas2 using NVIDIA GPUs, resulting in runtimes well under a minute for networks up to 1,000,000 nodes.

If RecViz is run on a system without a dedicated GPU, or one that does not have a NVIDIA GPU based on the Volta and Turing architectures or newer, then the graph service will use networkx’s single-threaded CPU version of ForceAtlas2 as a fallback, with equivalent settings meaning equivalent graphs would be output for the user, but much more slowly. Therefore, successfully implementing the caching feature represents another technical achievement that was critical for improving perceived performance on these systems, preventing the same graphs from being recomputed for repeated ﬁlter parameters.

It was initially thought the graph service could use networkx with the nx-cugraph backend, which dynamically replaces many of the networkx CPU-based algorithms with cuGraph accel-erated versions with zero code changes (NVIDIA 2025). However, it was found that nx-cugraph does not yet support ForceAtlas2, hence RAPIDS cuGraph had to be utilised instead, causing an implementation di"culty as two versions of the graph layout code were required. E!orts were made to reach out to the nx-cugraph maintainers via GitHub Issues with a feature request. Whilst a prompt and positive response to this feature request was received, work on the feature
27


did not conclude early enough for RecViz to forgo the split implementation of graph layout.

The fourth and ﬁnal task that the Graph object performs is to write the GEXF ﬁle of the graph to the cache directory, which is done by removing the interaction history array from the networkx graph object, which cannot be represented by the GEXF format and is replaced by a string representation, and then calling networkx’s write_gexf function, passing the networkx graph object and the cache path as arguments.

In order to meet requirement S3, the Graph object also provides a method for running the Louvain community detection algorithm, in order to extract a community structure from the networkx graph object that optimises modularity, which is a scalar value between -1 and 1 that measures the density of edges inside communities against edges outside communities (Blondel et al. 2008). A higher modularity indicates a more distinct community structure. Similarly to ForceAtlas2, the algorithm is made signiﬁcantly more usable for graphs with millions of nodes and edges when accelerated with cuGraph. NVIDIA testing on a graph with 1M nodes and 58M edges showed a runtime with cuGraph of ↓18 seconds, as opposed to ↓18 minutes with the CPU networkx implementation of Louvain, a ↓60x speedup (Ratzel and Yonemitsu 2024). Therefore, RecViz integrates the RAPIDS cuGraph version of Louvain and uses networkx as a fallback.

5.2.4	Recommendation Service

The recommendation service uses RecBole (due to its versatile feature set mentioned in Section 4.3.2) and works together with the dataset service to provide methods for loading pre-trained .pth format PyTorch recommender models, and then getting top-k recommendations from a loaded model for a speciﬁc user, or all users in a dataset at once.  In order to load a model, the recommendation service calls the get_dataset method of a dataset service object, which returns the Dataset object for the provided dataset name, if it is found. The get_models method of the Dataset object is then called to retrieve the list of valid model names mapped to the absolute paths of their model ﬁles.

If the requested model name is present, the RecBole load_data_and_model function is called to obtain the loaded Python object for the model, an object containing the RecBole representation of the dataset, and a RecBole conﬁg object containing training hyperparameters. The methods for getting top-k recommendations from the model for a speciﬁc user or all users, get_topk_all and get_topk_uid, both make use of a utility function implemented as get_topk. This function takes in an array of "internal" user IDs, which are retrieved by passing the "external" user IDs found in the dataset to the RecBole function token%id.

Another RecBole function, full_sort_topk, is then used to retrieve two lists of lists, where the elements are in the same order as the array of user IDs that was passed. The ﬁrst one contains lists of length k, representing the scores given by the model to the top-k recommendations. The second one contains lists of the the top-k recommended internal item IDs in descending sorted order. The code reformats these into a dictionary, mapping external user IDs, that are retrieved by passing the internal user IDs to RecBole’s id%token function, to a sorted list of pairs with the external item ID of each recommendation and the corresponding score. A diagram illustrating the relationship between user IDs, item IDs and scores is provided in Figure 5.4.

5.3	Frontend

This section will focus on what technologies and development tools were found most useful for implementation of the frontend components, and why they were useful. Any implementation di"culties or weaknesses introduced into the project by these technologies are also discussed.

5.3.1	React and Vite

Vite is a frontend web development server that supports developing React frontends. The most useful feature that it provided was Hot Module Replacement (HMR) (VoidZero 2019), which meant that whenever changes were made to React components in the IDE, these changes were reﬂected almost immediately in the browser, as the modiﬁed components are swapped out without having to restart the web app. This saved signiﬁcant time within the frontend implementation workﬂow and also allowed bugs to be spotted more quickly too, as the components being tested in the browser were always the latest versions. When switching over from Django to Vite, it was observed that the frontend server started up much faster than the Django development server, saving additional time.

5.3.2	react-sigma and SigmaJS

As RecViz uses SigmaJS, a JavaScript visualisation library that uses WebGL as opposed to HTML5 Canvas to make better use of the GPU for rendering graphs, integration with the React frontend would have been more complicated, without support for the React Context API. The use of react-sigma signiﬁcantly eased this process, by providing the SigmaContainer React component, which instantiates SigmaJS and shares it with child components automatically through a React context per SigmaContainer.  This works well in RecViz, as the frontend contains multiple SigmaJS graphs that have dependent child components like input event handlers. Whilst SigmaJS brought performance beneﬁts, it also introduces a moderate memory overhead when loading in the graph data, which increases the required amount of RAM to successfully load in graphs. Memory usage data is provided in Chapter 6.
29


5.3.3	Performance Optimisation and Challenges

In order to ensure good frontend performance when the browser is under load, it was important to identify and prevent unnecessary re-renders of the React components.  By default, React re-renders components in response to state changes. If this is done excessively, it can cause a degraded user experience by wasting compute cycles and noticeably delaying the frontend’s response to user input. During implementation, React’s memo API was used to tackle this.

When wrapping components in the memo API, their rendering is skipped if none of their properties have changed since the last render. This means that even if a parent component is re-rendered, any child components wrapped in memo will not re-render. Furthermore, refs are used to store frequently-changing information that should not trigger re-renders, such as the text in text input boxes. Problems with re-rendering were easily identiﬁed thanks to the use of a tool called React Scan (Million Software 2025), a drop-in JavaScript library that highlights when React components re-render using animated purple overlays. This tool also allowed monitoring of the rendering frame rate of the graphs.


5.4	Installation commands

During implementation, it was found that the correct versions of dependencies required to run RecBole and cuGraph were not always clearly stated in their online documentation, which slowed down the process. To this end, environment ﬁles and installation commands were provided for three supported system conﬁgurations, which are Windows without CUDA support, macOS without CUDA support, and Linux/WSL2 with CUDA support, as cuGraph relies on a conda environment on top of Linux. These environment ﬁles aid the set up of the correct pip and conda environments, and the project readme will walk the user through setting correct values for the environment variables used by RecViz.

6.4	Supervised User Evaluation

In order to provide further evidence of the extent to which RecViz has met its requirements, a supervised user evaluation was run in which 10 participants used RecViz to perform simple anticipated workﬂows. The study was carefully designed to comply fully with the School of Computing Science ethics checklist in Appendix A.

6.4.1	Methodology

The evaluation consists of two supervised experiments with a distinct set of steps that participants are walked through.  The ﬁrst experiment aims to test requirements M1, M3, M4, M5, S1, and S2. The second experiment aims to test requirements C1, M9, M10, S4, and C3. In total, the experiments are intended to take 20 minutes per participant. After both experiments, all participants ﬁll in a survey, the ﬁrst half of which consists of quantitative questions, and the second half consists of open-ended questions that aim to collect qualitative information on requirement M12 as well as general opinions of the application. Screen recordings and voice recordings are taken during both experiments to aid the collection of accurate timing data, then destroyed after the necessary data is gathered.
34


6.4.2	Experiment 1

10 participants were selected via convenience sampling from a population of ﬁnal-year University of Glasgow Computing Science Honours students who had never used or seen RecViz before. These participants are split into two groups of 5. One of these groups will receive a live demon-stration and explanation of the application before completing a multi-step task, whilst the other will attempt to complete the steps with no prior guidance. Users are subjected to 8 steps in the task, based on the requirements, that can be performed from within the ﬁrst UI layer of the application, which is the user-item interactions layer. The steps, which are included in Appendix B, are selected in order to try and test requirements that cannot immediately be discerned as met or not met during development, such as requirements M1, M5, and M9.

The experiment aims to collect four metrics that will conﬁrm the viability and intuitiveness of common workﬂows:
•  Time on step: how long it takes for the participant to get from the start to the end of a step
•  Time on task: how long it takes for the participant to get from the ﬁrst step of the task to the ﬁnal step of the task
•  Critical errors: errors that cause the participant to be unable to reach the ﬁnal step of the task without manual intervention by the researcher (this will be provided in this case)
•  Non-critical errors: errors which do not impact correct task completion and only slow down or inconvenience the participant
All instructions and the narration of the live demonstration, if used, are read from a pre-written script.

6.4.3	Experiment 2

In order to cover usage of the user comparison layer, the same participants, staying in the same groups, complete a second task, where they are subjected to 9 steps based on the requirements. The same four metrics are collected as in Experiment 1. The key di!erence is that 5 of the 9 steps are exclusively done within the user comparison layer. In order to account for possible confounding factors, the same machine is used as in Experiment 1 and this is kept consistent across participants. Furthermore, the experiment is held in the same room as Experiment 1 and participants always use the same input devices. Again, any instructions are read by the researcher from a pre-written script.

6.4.4	Study questionnaire

All participants answer a questionnaire containing quantitative questions of a similar style to those from the Questionnaire for User Interface Satisfaction. Two of the areas that QUIS targets are ease of use and ease of learning (Norman et al. 1998). This will form the basis for testing the level of user acceptance for the application interface.

6.4.5	Pilot Evaluation

It was important to validate that all parts of the user evaluation were able to run smoothly and that all instructions were su"ciently clear for the participants to follow. Therefore, the study was ﬁrst run on a small sample of 2 participants who did not participate in the ﬁnal sample, where one participant received the live training demonstration and the other did not.  It was found that a small number of the steps had minor errors (such as incorrect user/item IDs or typos which confused the participants) which were corrected before the ﬁnal evaluation. One of the

6.5	Requirements Review

In order to make a ﬁnal judgement on the success of the initial version of the RecViz system, the Must Have, Should Have, and Could Have requirements from Section 3.3 are enumerated. There were 15 Must Have requirements, all of which are deemed to have been met. Additionally, there were four Should Have requirements. Again, all of these are deemed to have been met. Two requirements were missed in the Could Have section. Out of the four total Could Have

requirements, two are deemed to have been met, while the other two were missed.

The two Could Have requirements that were missed were requirement C2 and requirement C4. Requirement C2, the timeline feature, was missed because performance issues were encountered in the initial working implementations. On smaller graphs of under 250,000 edges, switching between di!erent timestamps and scrubbing the timeline remained responsive. But when the graph size hit 1,000,000 edges, very slow performance was observed, where the visualisation would become unresponsive for six or seven seconds per timestamp change. This level of perfor-mance was deemed insu"cient for the ﬁnal product and there was not enough time to look into potential optimisations to resolve this.

Regarding requirement C4, the visualisation of user/item embeddings, it was deemed too tedious and time-consuming to handle the fact that some models allowed the retrieval of ﬁnal user/item embeddings in a representable form, whereas others did not. Furthermore, di"culties were en-countered trying to get RecBole to correctly retrieve embeddings. The RecBole documentation was found to be perhaps a little unintuitive in this regard.

All in all, it was found that 21 out of 23 target requirements were met, resulting in a fulﬁl-ment rate of about 91%. The relevant fulﬁlment justiﬁcations are provided in Appendix F.

7.1	Summary

RecViz is a novel web application designed for recommender systems researchers and developers to visualise datasets and compare recommender systems acting upon them. Datasets are primarily visualised via node-link graphs which are laid out such that more similar users or items are plotted visually closer together.

It leverages modern libraries and GPU acceleration to enable interactive data visualisation at scales that may be unfeasible with other software solutions. RecViz allows researchers and developers to swiftly access user and item attributes, and compare users within datasets with the ability to quickly calculate and view useful similarity metrics.

Performance testing demonstrated promising responsive interactivity even with million-scale datasets, such as the widely used MovieLens 1M benchmark dataset. User evaluation showed that the learning curve of the application was low, with most participants being able to perform common tasks with few to no errors, even without prior training, although there are deﬁnite ﬂaws to be addressed in future. Most participants found the visualisations to be understandable and aesthetically pleasing, and 90% of participants agreed that RecViz could be useful for comparing di!erent recommender systems.


7.2	Future Work

Other than fulﬁlling the missed requirements and addressing the UI ﬂaws that were identiﬁed in the user evaluation, there are exciting avenues for future work to build upon RecViz. Some could provide relatively easy performance gains, such as reducing the amount of storage and memory used by the graph data, through reducing the amount of data stored in the GEXF ﬁles. This could be combined with the use of a compression library such as pako (nodeca 2023).

Potentially large performance gains could be achieved in future if SigmaJS is replaced with a WebGPU-based graph renderer, rather than WebGL (Khronos 2025). WebGPU is an emerg-ing successor API to WebGL that better leverages modern GPUs to further raise the bar on performance (Mozilla 2025). WebGPU graph renderers exist today, but are not yet as mature or as well-documented as SigmaJS. In future it may be desirable to build a bespoke graph renderer for RecViz.

Future work could also focus on eliminating the reliance on CUDA, so that fast graph lay-out is available on non-NVIDIA GPUs, expanding the applicability of RecViz.  Work could
40


be done to add a ForceAtlas2 implementation that is written in Apple’s MLX array framework (Hannun et al. 2023). This would expand support for fast graph layout to Macs with Apple silicon.

The current architecture should be su"ciently ﬂexible to add to the explainability value of RecViz, through the implementation of visualisations for the exact steps that recommender models take to output predictions. Furthermore, in future it may be desirable to replace RecBole with another potentially custom library for increased ﬂexibility. Performance could be validated on larger datasets like MovieLens 20M (GroupLens 2015) that could not have ﬁt on the RAM of the test machine used in the evaluation, as well as multimodal datasets.

At the time of writing, the possible submission of a demonstration paper regarding the RecViz project to the ACM RecSys 2025 conference was being explored.


7.3	Reﬂection

Completing this project has both developed my individual resilience and reinforced to me the importance of good project management and software engineering practices.

I think that the most important learning from this process is the importance of pragmatism, exercised through narrowing down the scope of a project as quickly as possible, in order to cut down on excessive ideation periods that are often unproductive. Despite possible beneﬁt, the full creative process that one would want to follow cannot always be a!orded under time pressure.

A secondary learning was an increased appreciation for the importance of concise writing. This was also by some distance the largest academic writing piece I have worked on, and the most in-depth user evaluation I have run to date. The lessons learnt are invaluable, particularly time management skills as the project had considerable complexity relative to the available time.
